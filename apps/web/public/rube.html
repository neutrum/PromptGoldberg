A TRIBUTE

Rube Goldberg
1883–1970

The engineer who drew the most complicated ways to do the simplest things, and gave the world a name for them. This game carries his name in its own, as thanks.

The Self-Ringing Dinner Bellan invention in his spirit, drawn for this page
  1. A hungry cat pulls the string tied to its supper dish,
  2. which tips the shelf and lets the marble go.
  3. The marble rolls down the ramp
  4. and knocks over a row of dominoes.
  5. The last domino tips a cup of water
  6. into a bucket, which gets heavy, sinks and pulls the rope
  7. that rings the dinner bell. The cat, of course, was already in the kitchen.

The engineer who drew instead

  • 1883Reuben Garrett Lucius Goldberg is born on the Fourth of July in San Francisco. His father wants him to be an engineer.
  • 1904He graduates in engineering from the University of California, Berkeley, and goes to work mapping water mains and sewer pipes for the city of San Francisco.
  • Six months laterHe quits to draw sports cartoons for the San Francisco Chronicle, then the Bulletin.
  • 1907He moves to New York to cartoon for the Evening Mail, and becomes one of the best-known cartoonists in America.
  • 1912 onHis “invention” cartoons begin: enormous, deadpan contraptions that perform a trivial task in a dozen steps, each lettered and explained.
  • 1929–1931The inventions of Professor Lucifer Gorgonzola Butts run in Collier's Weekly. The professor's machines come with a straight face and a captioned list.
  • 1946He helps found the National Cartoonists Society and becomes its first president. Its top prize, the Reuben, is named after him; he wins it in 1967.
  • 1948He wins the Pulitzer Prize for editorial cartooning, for “Peace Today”, a warning about the atomic age.
  • 1949Students at Purdue University hold the first Rube Goldberg Machine Contest. Revived in 1983, it still runs today.
  • 1966His name enters the dictionary as an adjective, for anything fantastically complicated.
  • 1970He dies in New York on December 7, aged 87. In 1995 his Self-Operating Napkin appears on a U.S. postage stamp.

Why his machines still make us grin

A joke about engineering, by an engineer

He knew how machines worked, so the absurdity is precise: every step really would cause the next. The gag is the gap between all that ingenuity and the tiny job it does.

Cause and effect you can follow

Each step is lettered and explained. You trace the chain with your finger, and the last step lands like a punchline.

A mirror for modern life

He drew them as satire: we build complicated ways to do simple things. A hundred years on, that has not stopped being funny.

An invitation to build

Classrooms, contests, music videos and kitchen tables are full of real chain reactions built in his name. People don't only look at his machines; they want to make one.

From his drawings to Prompt Goldberg

Prompt Goldberg is our small way of saying thank you. You describe a place in one sentence, and the AI sets up a stage where a ball has to reach a goal. Then a person builds the chain: ramps, fans, cannons, dominoes, each step causing the next, run with real physics. The fun is his fun: watching a plan you made go off one step at a time, and laughing when it almost works.

The best chain reactions are the ones you build yourself, and watch go wrong, and fix, and run again.
